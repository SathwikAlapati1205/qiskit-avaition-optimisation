In plain English

What we actually did.

Planes bunch up when too many leave at once. We used a real quantum computer to find the best way to space them out.

The problem

At LAX, flights that leave in the same 15 minutes queue for the runway with engines running. Taxiing is about 46% of all landing and take-off CO2 in our data (99,711 of 214,796 tonnes, estimated), and each extra departure in the window adds 0.310 minutes of taxi time to every plane. In our 16-departure window, each of the six flights burns 4.65 extra minutes and about 194 kg of CO2 sitting in the queue: 1,165.83 kg of CO2 and 368.9 kg of fuel in total, for no benefit.

The fix we tested

Each flight waits at the gate with engines off for 0, 5, 10 or 15 minutes, so departures spread out and fewer planes sit in a queue with engines running. Waiting costs 0.20 per minute and burns no fuel, and a penalty of 40 forces exactly one wait choice per flight. Six flights with four choices each is 4,096 schedules, stored in 12 qubits. The goal: the schedule with the lowest congestion CO2 plus wait cost.

What we achieved

The IBM quantum computer found the lowest-cost schedule: hold all six flights for 5 minutes. That removes 1,165.83 kg of CO2, 368.9 kg of fuel and 27.9 minutes of engines-running taxi time, for a wait cost of only 6.00. Cost fell 194 times, and the busy window dropped from 16 departures to 8.

Honest context: with six flights a normal computer can also check all 4,096 schedules instantly. We did that to prove the quantum answer is correct. The value is the method, which needs half the qubits and a small number of runs, and is built for schedules too large to check one by one.

Why quantum

Why the quantum computer chose 5 minutes.

The drop from 16 departures to 8 is the result. The reason is the cost of each flight, which the quantum circuit weighs across all 4,096 schedules at once.

Our own algorithm

We wrote our own method. It uses half the qubits.

We did not use a ready-made solver. We designed how the problem is stored on the quantum computer and how its settings are tuned. Fewer qubits and fewer runs mean a cheaper, less error-prone job on real hardware.

12

Compact qubit encoding

Each flight's hold (0, 5, 10, 15 min) is stored in just 2 qubits, instead of 4 in the standard method. Six flights need 12 qubits, not 24.

p=1

Shallowest QAOA circuit

QAOA is the quantum recipe we run, and we use a single layer, the shortest version. Fewer steps means less room for hardware noise to corrupt the answer.

89

Two-phase CVaR tuning

We test 64 settings across a wide range, then zoom into the best area with 25 finer ones. Each run is scored on its best 25% of results (CVaR), so noise counts for less.

The answer

The best schedule: hold all six flights for 5 minutes.

The quantum computer returned 12 bits. Read them in pairs, one pair per flight: 00 = no hold, 01 = 5 min, 10 = 10 min, 11 = 15 min. A classical check of all 4,096 schedules confirms it is the best one.
